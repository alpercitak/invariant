## What to look for

The bright bands show where the combined wave has large swings. Along the darkest lines, a crest from one source lines up with a trough from the other and the waves cancel. Nearby, they cancel only partly. The diamond marks the midpoint, which is the same distance from both sources.

At 0°, the waves leave the sources in step and reach the midpoint in step. At 180°, source B is half a cycle ahead, so the two waves cancel there. Shorter wavelengths put more cycles into the same difference in travel distance, so the bands crowd together.

## The rule

At each point, add the two waves. If each alone has amplitude **A**, the largest combined amplitude is **2A**. The map measures the combined amplitude as a fraction of that maximum.

Here **a** is the relative amplitude from 0 to 1, and **Δr** is the distance from A minus the distance from B, in meters. **λ** is the wavelength in meters. **φ** is how far source B leads source A in phase. The control shows degrees and the equation uses radians, with 180° equal to π radians.

## What the map assumes

The two sources have the same frequency and a fixed phase difference. Their waves add linearly, and each wave keeps the same amplitude as it travels. Real waves can weaken with distance or reflect off barriers. Unequal amplitudes can leave some motion even when the two waves arrive half a cycle apart.

## Where the idea appears

Two speakers playing the same steady tone can sound louder in one spot and quieter a few steps away. Changing where you stand changes the two travel distances and the phase difference where the sound reaches you.
